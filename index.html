Research

Working Papers

The Informal Safety Net: Extended Families, Intra-Household Bargaining, and Female Labor Supply
PDF
Existing studies have documented child penalties in women’s labor market outcomes around the world and the importance of childcare support in women’s career paths. Yet access to childcare is often limited in developing countries, and women rely on extended family networks. Using an event-study on Chinese mini-census data, I show that extended-family women recover faster from childbirth than nuclear-family women; conversely, they face a steeper decline in labor force participation upon marriage than those in nuclear living arrangements. To understand the mechanisms through which living arrangement shapes women’s decision-making, I develop a dynamic equilibrium model in which family formation is chosen endogenously in the marriage market and labor supplies are determined by collective household decision-making. Counterfactual exercises show that the One-Child Policy in China has an unexpected intergenerational effect: by reducing the number of siblings, it has increased the likelihood of young couples coresiding, which in turn provides informal childcare support and mitigates the child penalty on women’s labor market outcomes.
Nonparametric Identification of Dynamic Collective Models
PDF
In dynamic collective models with limited commitment, spouses renegotiate the allocation of resources whenever one of them would otherwise prefer to leave the marriage, so the Pareto weight that summarizes bargaining power evolves over time. This weight is rarely observed. We show that the dynamic structure of the model is itself a source of identification. Treating the Pareto weight as a latent Markov state whose noisy measurements are the household's choices, we build on identification results for dynamic models with unobserved state variables from the nonclassical measurement error literature. With panel data on hours (recorded with error), wages, assets and aggregate consumption, the joint distribution of allocations and the Pareto weight is identified up to the cardinalization of each spouse's utility, without distribution factors, assignable goods or individual consumption data. The distribution of bargaining power across households, its evolution, and the occurrence and direction of renegotiation follow, as does the sharing rule up to an additive constant. The law of motion of the weight further identifies the primitives of the limited-commitment marriage contract: the probability that the agreement survives at each state and the distribution of each spouse's reservation weight. These objects deliver a test of full commitment that requires no distribution factors and locates where in the state space, and through whose participation constraint, commitment fails.
